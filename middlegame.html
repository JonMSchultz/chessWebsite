MAKE A PLAN

Middlegame strategy.

The middlegame is where piece activity, pawn structure, king safety, and tactical awareness come together. Learn to make plans instead of random moves.

⚔

Forks

A fork is a move that attacks two or more enemy pieces at the same time. Knights are especially effective fork attackers because of their unusual movement.

Look for: A knight check that also attacks the queen, or a pawn move that attacks two pieces.
⟋

Pins

A pin restricts a piece because moving it would expose a more valuable piece behind it. An absolute pin exposes the king; a relative pin exposes another valuable target.

Look for: Pieces lined up on the same rank, file, or diagonal.
↗

Skewers

A skewer attacks a valuable piece first. When it moves out of the line of attack, a less valuable piece behind it can be captured.

Look for: A rook, bishop, or queen attacking two pieces on one line.
✣

Discovered attacks & double attacks

A discovered attack happens when one piece moves and reveals an attack from a piece behind it. A double attack creates two threats with one move.

Look for: Moves that uncover a rook, bishop, or queen while creating a second threat.
♜

Deflection, decoys & removing the defender

These tactical ideas manipulate the opponent's pieces. Deflection pulls a defender away; a decoy lures a piece to a bad square; removing the defender captures or drives away a key guard.

Look for: A defender protecting several important squares or pieces.
◎

Control the center

Central squares give pieces access to both sides of the board. Pawns and pieces that influence the center often gain greater mobility and make it harder for the opponent to organize.

Practical idea: Develop pieces toward useful central squares rather than moving the same piece repeatedly.
♘

Development & piece activity

Develop your minor pieces, connect your rooks, and place pieces where they influence important squares. A piece is active when it has useful options and targets.

Practical idea: Ask which of your pieces is least active and how it can improve.
▦

Pawn structure, weak squares & outposts

Pawns shape the position. Doubled, isolated, or backward pawns can become targets. An outpost is a strong square—often for a knight—that cannot easily be challenged by an enemy pawn.

Practical idea: Identify pawn weaknesses before choosing where to place your pieces.
♔

Open and closed positions & king safety

Open positions have more open lines and often favor long-range pieces. Closed positions have locked pawn structures and can favor maneuvering. Keep your king safe and be aware of open files and diagonals near it.

Practical idea: Match your plan to the pawn structure and the safety of both kings.
⇄

When to exchange pieces

Exchanges are not automatically good or bad. Consider whether the trade improves your pawn structure, removes a strong defender, reduces attacking pressure, or makes a favorable endgame.

Ask yourself: Who benefits from the resulting position?
⌖

Identify weaknesses and create threats

Look for undefended pieces, weak pawns, exposed kings, and squares the opponent cannot easily defend. Build pressure before forcing a tactic.

Ask yourself: What is my opponent's most difficult problem to solve?
☷

Make a plan, then calculate

Choose a target, improve the pieces that support it, and consider the opponent's best response. Before committing, check forcing moves: checks, captures, and direct threats.

Simple routine: Identify threats → evaluate weaknesses → improve your worst piece → calculate candidate moves.
